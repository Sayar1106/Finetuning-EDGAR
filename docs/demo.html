NFLX is the real failure. It parses and all four numbers are right, but category F1 is 42 and match F1 is 11 — the model found risks the teacher labels did not pair with. An 8B adapter matches the frontier model on content — identical numeric accuracy, category F1 within two points — and beats it three-to-one on emitting JSON that parses without repair. It does not beat it on cost: measured at 58 seconds per filing on a rented A100, inference works out to $0.0256 against Sonnet's $0.0283. That is parity, not the twentieth this project originally claimed, and the claim was withdrawn rather than softened.

What the numbers do not cover

Twelve filings is a small test set. Every figure on this page carries a 95% bootstrap confidence interval in the checked-in reports, resampled over filings because fields within one filing are correlated. Category F1 of 77.3 has an interval of [68.0, 85.4].

The risk labels come from a teacher model, and the teacher is the ceiling. Categories and summaries were generated by Claude Haiku 4.5 and audited against a blind human sample — 60 risks across 15 filings. Blind agreement with the human reviewer was 72%. So 77.3 should be read against roughly that, not against perfection. The four financial figures have no such problem: they come from SEC XBRL companyfacts and are exact.

Greedy decoding is not bit-reproducible across hardware. Re-running these same three configurations on an A40 with a newer transformers build moved numeric accuracy to 100.0% and category F1 to 80.0%, and made one base-model filing parse strictly. Different kernels round differently, one argmax flips, and the generation diverges. The figures published here are the lower of the two runs.
